\begin{abstract}
IDK cascades chain progressively heavier classifiers so easy inputs exit early. Two recent improvements skip the middle classifier when the first stage is unlikely to be refined: Nguyen et al.\ (RTSS 2024) used a static confidence threshold; Katikaneni et al.\ (RTSS 2025) replaced it with a Random Forest on three softmax features. We ask whether a much simpler predictor suffices. Under Katikaneni et al.'s cross-dataset ImageNet-V2 protocol and prediction target (will the middle classifier abstain?), a two-feature logistic regression on \textit{confidence} and \textit{margin} matches the Random Forest on the first-stage-IDK test population: 365.3 vs.\ 363.8~ms/image at 55.3\% vs.\ 55.3\% top-1 accuracy on a Jetson Orin Nano CPU. The paired bootstrap 95\% CI of the per-image accuracy difference includes zero; the latency CI shows the Random Forest ahead by at most 2.6~ms --- \emph{less than its own 3.8~ms median per-decision evaluation cost}, which the logistic undercuts by a measured $205\times$ (19~$\mu$s at batch size 1). Unlike the tree ensemble, the deployed logistic is branch-free and data-independent, so its WCET is immediate to bound --- the operative property where the skip predictor's WCET itself enters the schedulability budget.
\end{abstract}

real-time machine learning, IDK cascades, anytime classification, edge inference, schedulability

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Modern real-time systems increasingly rely on deep neural networks for perception, control, and classification at the edge. The dominant tension in this setting is well known: more accurate networks have larger and more variable inference times, which makes them difficult to schedule under hard deadlines. \textit{IDK cascades}, introduced by Wang et al.\ \cite{wang2017idk} and later formalized for real-time schedulability analysis by Baruah et al.\ \cite{baruah2023optimal}, address this tension by chaining a sequence of classifiers $A \to B \to C$ of increasing accuracy and cost. At each stage, the classifier either commits to a prediction or returns ``IDK'' (I-Don't-Know), in which case the next, heavier classifier is invoked. Easy inputs exit at $A$; only difficult inputs incur the full $A+B+C$ cost.

A recurring observation in the IDK-cascade literature is that the middle classifier $B$ is often \textit{wasted execution}: when $A$ already returns IDK with very low confidence, $B$ is unlikely to do better, and the cascade should jump directly to the deterministic final classifier $C$. Nguyen, Cheng, and Carroll \cite{nguyen2024probabilistic} formalized this observation with a probabilistic profiling phase and a single static threshold (skip $B$ when $A$'s top-1 confidence falls below 0.3), reporting 11.5--17.0\% average latency reduction across three ImageNet-V2 subsets with no loss of accuracy. Katikaneni, Cheng, and Carroll \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest} argued that a single static threshold does not generalize across datasets, and replaced it with a Random Forest trained on three features of $A$'s softmax distribution: \textit{confidence}, \textit{entropy}, and \textit{margin}. They showed that the learned skip predictor outperforms the static threshold on three ImageNet-V2 subsets and on a Jetson Nano embedded target.

The Random Forest, however, introduces its own per-image inference cost. The 2025 paper itself flags as future work the question of whether a simpler tabular model would suffice. In this work-in-progress, we take that question literally: \textit{is a two-feature logistic skip rule sufficient to recover most of the latency benefit reported in \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest}, while remaining interpretable and trivially cheap to evaluate at inference time?}

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) We reproduce the dynamic-threshold cascade of \cite{nguyen2024probabilistic} with off-the-shelf pretrained ResNet-18/34/152 weights on ImageNet-V2. (ii) We implement a logistic-regression skip predictor over $\{$\textit{confidence}, \textit{margin}$\}$ and compare it to both the static threshold and a re-implementation of the Random Forest baseline with the same hyperparameters, under the cross-dataset protocol of \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest} (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}), with bootstrap interval estimates and a nested-feature ablation. (iii) We measure the per-decision overhead of the skip predictor itself on an embedded ARM target and argue that, beyond its measured cost, the logistic's branch-free evaluation is the more analyzable term to carry into a schedulability bound.

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{IDK Cascades}
A formal IDK cascade \cite{baruah2023optimal} is a sequence of classifiers $C_1, \dots, C_n$ ordered by non-decreasing average execution time $\bar{e}_i$ and accuracy $a_i$; each $C_i$ with $i < n$ may abstain by emitting an ``IDK'' token $\bot$, and $C_n$ must always commit. Let $p_i \in [0,1]$ denote the probability that $C_i$ emits $\bot$ on a randomly drawn input. The cascade's expected per-input response time is $\mathbb{E}[R] = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \bar{e}_i \prod_{j<i} p_j$, and the schedulability question is whether the \emph{worst-case} response time, $R_{\text{wc}} = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_i^{\text{wc}}$, satisfies $R_{\text{wc}} \le D$ for a deadline $D$. Baruah et al.\ \cite{baruah2023optimal} give an optimal-synthesis algorithm for cascade ordering when both $\bar{e}_i$ and $p_i$ are known, treating the cascade as a static structure: whether to invoke $C_{i+1}$ after an IDK at $C_i$ is fixed at design time.

\subsection{Static Threshold Skipping (Nguyen et al., 2024)}
Nguyen, Cheng, and Carroll \cite{nguyen2024probabilistic} observed that on ImageNet-V2 TopImages, when ResNet-18 returns a top-1 softmax probability below 0.3, ResNet-34 \emph{itself outputs IDK} in more than 92\% of cases. Since time is only saved by skipping $B$ when $B$ would output IDK, their dynamic cascade skips ResNet-34 whenever ResNet-18's confidence falls below 0.3, jumping directly to ResNet-152; they report 11.5--17.0\% average latency reduction across the three ImageNet-V2 subsets (13.8\% on Matched-Frequency, GTX~1070~Ti) with no measurable accuracy loss.

\subsection{Random Forest Skipping (Katikaneni et al., 2025)}
Katikaneni, Cheng, and Carroll \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest} replaced the single threshold with a Random Forest classifier (\texttt{n\_estimators=50}, \texttt{max\_depth=4}, \texttt{class\_weight='balanced'}) trained on three features of the ResNet-18 softmax output $\mathbf{s}$ (with order statistics $s_{(1)} \ge s_{(2)} \ge \dots$): \textbf{confidence} $\mathrm{conf} = s_{(1)}$, the top-1 probability; \textbf{entropy} $-\sum_i s_i \log s_i$; and \textbf{margin} $s_{(1)} - s_{(2)}$, the top-1/top-2 gap.
The label is whether ResNet-34 will itself output IDK. They train on the $A$-abstained samples of Matched-Frequency and TopImages and test on Threshold-0.7. On a Jetson Nano 4~GB embedded target (CPU mode), Random Forest skipping beat both the no-skip and static-threshold cascades on all three ImageNet-V2 subsets; on a desktop-class CPU the static threshold retained an edge on one subset.

\section{Approach}
\label{sec:approach}

\subsection{Cascade Architecture}
We use the canonical three-stage cascade from \cite{nguyen2024probabilistic, katikaneni2025randomforest}, $A = \text{ResNet-18} \to B = \text{ResNet-34} \to C = \text{ResNet-152}$, all loaded with default \texttt{torchvision} pretrained weights (\texttt{IMAGENET1K\_V1}). A classifier $C_i$ emits $\bot$ (IDK) when its top-1 softmax probability is below an accuracy threshold $\tau_{\text{acc}} = 0.65$, matching the Nguyen et al.\ setup; the final classifier $C$ never abstains. The control variable $\mathrm{skip}(\mathbf{x}) \in \{0, 1\}$ is evaluated only when $A$ returns $\bot$: if $\mathrm{skip}=1$ the cascade jumps directly to $C$ and commits, otherwise it runs $B$ and falls through to $C$ only if $B$ also abstains.

\subsection{Skip-Decision Variants}
We compare four implementations of $\mathrm{skip}(\cdot)$:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline}: $\mathrm{skip}(\cdot) \equiv 0$. Always run $B$ after $A$ abstains.
    \item \textbf{Static threshold} \cite{nguyen2024probabilistic}: $\mathrm{skip}(\mathbf{x}) = 1$ iff $\mathrm{conf}(A) < 0.3$.
    \item \textbf{Random Forest} (re-implementation of \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest} with the same hyperparameters): a 50-tree, depth-4 RF classifier on $\{\text{conf}, \text{entropy}, \text{margin}\}$, trained with \texttt{class\_weight='balanced'} and \texttt{min\_samples\_leaf=40}.
    \item \textbf{Logistic (ours)}: a logistic regression on two features $\{\text{conf}, \text{margin}\}$ only.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Logistic Skip Rule}
With $\mathrm{conf}(A)$ and $\mathrm{margin}(A)$ as defined in Sec.~\ref{sec:related}-C, the skip predictor is
\[
P(\mathrm{skip}=1 \mid \mathbf{x}) = \sigma\!\left(w_0 + w_1\,\mathrm{conf}(A) + w_2\,\mathrm{margin}(A)\right),
\]
with $\sigma(z) = (1 + e^{-z})^{-1}$. The training label is $y = 1$ when $B$ itself would abstain, i.e.\ $\max \mathbf{s}_B < \tau_{\text{acc}}$. This is the predictor target of both prior papers \cite{nguyen2024probabilistic, katikaneni2025randomforest}: time is only saved by skipping $B$ when $B$ would output IDK; if $B$ would commit, running it is the cheapest path to a committed answer ($t_B < t_C$). Both learned predictors are trained on this label. We fit on the subset of images where $A$ returned $\bot$ (the only inputs where $\mathrm{skip}$ is consulted), drawn from the two training subsets under the cross-dataset protocol of Section~\ref{sec:setup}, and apply \texttt{class\_weight='balanced'} to handle the asymmetric prior ($y=1$ for 66.9\% of $A$-IDK training images). At inference, the cascade skips $B$ when $P(\mathrm{skip}=1\mid \mathbf{x}) > 0.5$; evaluating the predictor reduces to two multiplications, two additions, and one sigmoid.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Datasets and Cross-Dataset Protocol}
We use all three ImageNet-V2 subsets \cite{recht2019imagenetv2} (10\,000 images each, 1\,000 classes): \textit{Matched-Frequency}, \textit{TopImages}, and \textit{Threshold-0.7}, obtained as the original per-variant release files of Recht et al.\ via the Hugging Face Hub, with the variant identity of each split verified against canonical standalone top-1 accuracies. Following the cross-dataset protocol of Katikaneni et al.\ \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest}, both learned predictors are trained on the first-stage-IDK samples of Matched-Frequency + TopImages and evaluated entirely on Threshold-0.7: train and test datasets are disjoint, so no within-subset split is needed, the full $A$-IDK test population is used, and the transfer property that motivated \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest} is tested directly.

\subsection{Hardware}
All inference is run in CPU mode (\texttt{torch.device("cpu")}), matching the GPU-disabled methodology of Katikaneni et al.\ \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest}. CPU is the operative regime: a single ResNet-152 GPU run is already cheap, so the cascade's value vanishes there. All measurements are taken on an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano (ARM Cortex-A78AE, CPU mode), the successor of the Jetson Nano used by \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest}: the board runs all model inference (cached softmax outputs and per-image stage timings) as well as the predictor training, evaluation, and per-decision cost profiling.

\subsection{Implementation}
Models are loaded from \texttt{torchvision.models} with default pretrained weights. Per-image timings are measured with \texttt{time.perf\_counter()} around the inference call only (preprocessing is excluded). Each measurement is the mean of five trials per image after a 50-image warm-up.

\subsection{Metrics and Evaluation Populations}
We report \textbf{average latency} (mean wall-clock ms/image over the stated population), \textbf{top-1 accuracy} of the cascade's final committed prediction, \textbf{skip rate} (fraction of the population on which $B$ was skipped), and, for the learned predictors, precision/recall/F1 of the skip decision plus measured per-decision cost. Two populations appear in Section~\ref{sec:results}. The threshold sweep uses the \emph{full 10\,000-image Matched-Frequency set} (a reproduction of \cite{nguyen2024probabilistic}). The learned-predictor comparisons use the \emph{$A$-IDK population of Threshold-0.7}: the skip rule is consulted nowhere else, and no Threshold-0.7 image appears in training. Numbers from the two populations are not directly comparable (the $A$-IDK population contains only hard inputs, so its latencies are higher and accuracies lower than full-set values). All point estimates on the test population are reported with bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals (10\,000 percentile-bootstrap resamples of the test population); comparisons between the Random Forest and the logistic use \emph{paired} bootstrap CIs of the per-image differences.

\subsection{Measured Predictor Overhead}
Table~\ref{tab:overhead} reports the per-decision cost of each learned predictor, measured at batch size 1 over 1\,000 test samples after warm-up (\texttt{time.perf\_counter}, on the Jetson Orin Nano): the scikit-learn Random Forest via \texttt{predict()}, the logistic in its deployed form (a NumPy dot product plus sigmoid). The median gap is $205\times$ (3.80~ms vs.\ 19~$\mu$s). Because worst-case behavior is what enters a schedulability bound, we also report the empirical p99 and maximum (6.37~ms vs.\ 48~$\mu$s). This measures the deployed scikit-learn implementation --- a compiled tree ensemble would narrow the gap --- but the logistic's two-multiply evaluation is a floor no 50-tree ensemble reaches. Beyond magnitude, the two predictors differ in \emph{analyzability}: the deployed logistic is branch-free and data-independent (exactly two multiplications, two additions, and one sigmoid on every input), so a WCET bound is immediate, whereas a tree ensemble's evaluation path is input-dependent and its interpreted implementation resists tight static bounding. The cascade latencies in Table~\ref{tab:variants} \emph{exclude} predictor cost; on the $A$-IDK population, where the predictor is consulted on every image, charging each variant its median per-decision cost adds 3.80~ms to the Random Forest row but only 0.02~ms to the logistic row --- \emph{more than the Random Forest's entire paired latency advantage over the logistic} ($\le 2.6$~ms). End-to-end, the cheapest learned rule is the logistic.

\section{Discussion and Future Work}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Why so few features suffice.} On a $K{=}1000$-class output, the bulk of entropy lives in the long tail of small probabilities; whether $B$ will abstain is dominated by $A$'s top-1 probability. The nested-model ablation (Table~\ref{tab:skip-f1}) makes this concrete: under cross-dataset transfer, neither margin nor entropy improves on confidence alone outside the bootstrap CIs. The interesting corollary is that a one-feature logistic \emph{is} a confidence threshold whose cut point is learned from training data --- so the practical gap between \cite{nguyen2024probabilistic} and \cite{katikaneni2025randomforest} is not the classifier family but whether the threshold is calibrated per deployment.

\textbf{When does skipping pay?} A skip saves $t_B$ when $B$ would abstain and wastes $t_C - t_B$ when $B$ would have committed, so a skip rule breaks even at precision $p^\ast = 1 - t_B/t_C$. On our board ($t_B \approx 97$~ms, $t_C \approx 339$~ms), $p^\ast \approx 0.71$, and both learned predictors operate at precision $\approx 0.77$ (Table~\ref{tab:skip-f1}) --- barely above break-even, which is why their aggressive skipping (Table~\ref{tab:variants}) yields only $\sim$3\% latency gains. The stage-cost ratio, not the predictor, is the operative variable: larger $t_C/t_B$ raises $p^\ast$, while cheap final stages make even mediocre skip rules profitable.

\textbf{Real-time implications.} The skip predictor's own WCET enters the schedulability budget. Our measured per-decision cost gap is $205\times$ at the median and $133\times$ at the empirical maximum (Table~\ref{tab:overhead}), and the logistic's branch-free, data-independent evaluation admits an immediate WCET bound where the tree ensemble's input-dependent path does not; in Baruah's framing \cite{baruah2023optimal}, where each stage's WCET is charged against the response-time bound, the near-zero-cost predictor is preferable whenever cascade accuracy and latency are comparable --- which Table~\ref{tab:variants} shows they are, before the Random Forest is even charged its own overhead.

\textbf{Future work.} Confidence is well-defined for any softmax classifier, so the rule is modality-agnostic (e.g., audio pipelines \cite{yantosca2025phonotomizer}); natural follow-ons are folding the predictor's WCET and $p^\ast$ into Baruah's response-time bound and multi-stage skip rules for longer cascades.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Under Katikaneni et al.'s cross-dataset protocol, measured end to end on a Jetson Orin Nano, a two-feature logistic skip rule matches the Random Forest's cascade accuracy and concedes at most 2.6~ms of cascade latency --- less than the Random Forest's own 3.8~ms median per-decision cost, which the logistic undercuts $205\times$ with a branch-free evaluation whose WCET is immediate to bound. The feature ablation shows confidence alone suffices, making the learned rule a dataset-calibrated threshold; the break-even precision $p^\ast = 1 - t_B/t_C$ explains when any skip rule pays. Folding both into an end-to-end schedulability analysis is the immediate next step.
